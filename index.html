5 steps for any situation · S N E J F

  1. Spot who decides, what's at stake, why it's ethical (effects on others + choice + seen as ethical).
  2. Name 2–3 course concepts, define each in one line.
  3. Explain why it happens: individual, issue or organisation.
  4. Judge with at least 2 normative theories.

Explain with 3 sources (Kish-Gephart 2010)

  • Bad case = the issue (moral intensity, framing).

Phrases that score

  3. The theory that explains it best.

S1 · Foundations of business ethics

Day 135 minCrane & Matten ch.1 · Melé

Goal: define business ethics, separate it from law, explain why context (region, organisation, globalization) changes it.

Definition

Business ethics = the study of business situations, activities and decisions where issues of right and wrong are addressed.

  • "Right/wrong" = morally, not commercially or financially.
  • Applies to all organisations, not only companies.
  • A form of applied ethics (Velasquez).

Oxymoron? No

  • Scandals → "bad" ethics are still ethics of a sort.
  • Business needs honesty and trust: impossible if directors always lied or buyers never trusted sellers.
  • Fairness makes people feel rewarded.
  • Not naïve: a systematic study of why acts are right or wrong.

Class test: Cynicism Quotient, 10 items, max 50 (higher = more cynical).

3 levels of issues

Systemic
The whole system (state tolerating bribery) → many groups acting together.
Corporate
One firm's culture/policies → company solutions.
Individual
One person → individual decisions; weak against the other two.

Ethics ≠ law

Law = institutionalization or codification of ethics into rules. Business ethics mostly lives where the law ends (the grey area).

Morality → ethics → theory

Morality: norms, values, beliefs in social processes defining right/wrong for a person or community.

Ethics: the study of morality, using reason to find rules and principles.

Ethical theories: the codification of those rules.

Morality→Ethics→Theory→Solution

Why it matters

  • Power of business in society.
  • Only ~48% of executives think firms contribute positively (McKinsey 2006).
  • Harm from malpractice (Rana Plaza 2013).
  • Stakeholder demands more complex.
  • Pressure on employees.
  • Trust deficit.
  • Ethics industry: UK ethical market ≈ £78bn/yr.

Globalization

Ongoing integration of political, social and economic interactions at the transnational level, regardless of physical distance.

Cultural
Contradictory demands. IKEA removed women from the Saudi catalogue.
Legal
Laws stop at borders: deterritorialization (Scholte 2005).
Account­ability
Walmart revenue > GDP of Australia, S. Korea, India (Myers 2016).

4 forces of globalization

  • Homogenizing: one global culture.
  • North American influence: the field is a US invention.
  • International variety.
  • Cultural diversity (Africa, Asia).

Balanced position & future

  • Many stakeholders, shareholders included.
  • Both individuals and corporations responsible.
  • Judge acts within the wider framework of business.
  • Future: convergence (democracy, human rights, anti-corruption); end the separation thesis (Freeman 1994: ethics vs economics); virtues matter.

Africa & Asia

Ubuntu (Africa): community, "I am because we are". Guanxi (China): relationship networks. Keiretsu (Japan), chaebol (Korea). Africa / Latin America: NGOs ("third sector") are key actors.

S2 · Sustainability, CSR & stakeholders

Day 135 minCrane & Matten ch.2

Goal: argue whether firms have responsibilities beyond profit, with Carroll, the business vs moral case and stakeholder theory.

Sustainability

Sustainable development (Brundtland 1987): meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own → intergenerational equity.

Sustainability: long-term maintenance of systems according to environmental, economic and social considerations.

Problems: pollution, waste, community damage from closures and outsourcing.

Triple bottom line (Elkington 1999)

Environ.
Conserve resources, biodiversity, limit pollution.
Economic
Earth's limits + firm's impact on the economy: bribes, tax avoidance.
Social
Justice: 80% of GDP for 1bn people, 20% for 6bn.

TBL = a change in how firms think, not just metrics. Are we expecting too much?

SDGs & critics

17 UN goals need firms + governments + NGOs; CSR as catalyst.

  • Growth contradiction; growth ≠ less poverty (Hickel 2015)
  • Inequality dropped from the text
  • Ignore unfair trade, speculation, tax evasion, debt
  • Poverty line $1.25 vs ~$5
  • No link to human rights / democracy
  • SDG-wash: 69% integrate, 56% suspect washing

What is a corporation?

  • Artificial person, legally independent, perpetual succession.
  • Owns its assets; shareholders own shares.
  • Managers have a fiduciary duty to shareholders.

Friedman: profit only

"The social responsibility of business is to increase its profits."

  1. Only humans are morally responsible.
  2. Other goals betray shareholders.
  3. Social issues = the state's job.

Firms ARE morally responsible

Legal identity
contracts, law
Agency
internal decision structure
Culture
shared beliefs shape people
Functional identity
act as persons

Carroll's pyramid

CSR = meeting the economic, legal, ethical and philanthropic demands of a society at a given time.

Philanthropic · desired
Ethical · expected
Legal · required
Economic · required

Bottom-up: E · L · E · P Conflicts between levels = dilemmas.

Why CSR? Business case

Enlightened self-interest: CSR when it serves the firm.

  • ↑ long-term revenues
  • ↓ costs (Walmart ~$1bn)
  • Manage risk (after Rana Plaza)
  • Social licence to operate (Mondelez)

Why CSR? Moral case

  • Externalities: responsible for side effects.
  • Power: use it responsibly.
  • Dependency: firms depend on stakeholders.

Explicit vs implicit CSR

Explicit · USANamed, voluntary firm activity

Principles + processes + outcomes:

Policies

Programmes

Impacts

Stakeholder theory

Stakeholder: individual or group harmed by, or benefiting from, the corporation, or whose rights it should respect.

  • Evan & Freeman 1993: principle of corporate rights + corporate effect.
  • Rowley 1997: network model.
  • Stockholder → stakeholder: legal (laws already bind) + economic (agency problem: short-term shareholders).

3 forms: normative (should) · descriptive (do) · instrumental (does it pay).

Corporate citizenship

Firm as political actor where governments retreat, fail or can't reach.

Limited view = philanthropy. Extended view = governing citizens' rights:

Social
providing / ignoring (M&S "Marks and Start")
Civil
enabling / disabling
Political
channelling / blocking

Accountability & transparency

Accountability: whether a firm is answerable for the consequences of its actions.

Transparency: decisions and impacts made visible: disclosure + clarity + accuracy.

S3 · Normative ethical theories

Day 245 minKey for Q4

Goal: explain and apply every theory, know its weaknesses, and use them together (pluralism).

Normative theory

The rules and principles that determine right and wrong for a given situation.

Business decisions need a rational argument that can be defended to stakeholders.

3 positions (De George 1999)

Absolutism
Universal, objective principles (Western theories).
Relativism
Morality depends on person + culture. ≠ descriptive relativism (just observing differences).
Pluralism ✓
Middle ground: accept diversity but agree on basic principles. Theories = tools.

Kaler 1999b: morality is a social phenomenon (variety) and about harm & benefit (so not "anything goes").

Context

Europe vs US: institutional vs individual morality · questioning vs accepting capitalism · justifying vs applying norms.

Religion vs theory: faith vs reason; spiritual vs social consequences.

Two families: consequentialist = teleological ("goal"), judge outcomes · non-consequentialist = deontological ("duty"), judge principles.

C Egoism

Right if decision-makers freely pursue their short-term desires or long-term interests.

  • Smith's invisible hand: self-interest + competition → social good.
  • Egoism ≠ selfishness (charity for reputation is egoism).
  • Enlightened egoism: invest in society for long-term interest.
  • Weakness: needs perfect markets (inequality, future generations).

Smith, Friedman

C Utilitarianism

Right if it gives the greatest good for the greatest number.

  • Bentham, Mill. Utility: hedonistic, eudemonistic, ideal.
  • ≈ cost-benefit analysis (impact studies, Rosia Montana).
  • Act = single act · Rule = type of act long-term.

3 problems: subjectivity · quantification · distribution (minorities).

Case · Ford Pinto

Gas tank could burst in rear crashes at 20 mph. Fix: $11 × 12.5M cars = $137.5M > priced deaths and injuries → no fix. Legal at the time.

Shows: pricing lives, victims as minority, over-reliance on numbers; Gioia's blind spot (S4).

D Kant · ethics of duties

Unchangeable obligations deduced a priori by reason, applied to all cases.

Categorical imperative · 3 tests C · H · U

  1. Consistency: everyone, always, no exception for me.
  2. Human dignity: never treat people merely as a means.
  3. Universality: acceptable to all rational people.

Problems: ignores outcomes · complexity · optimism (assumes rationality).

D Rights & justice

Locke: natural rights to life, freedom, property.

Human rights: basic, inalienable entitlements of all humans; create duties for others.

Powerful (UN Global Compact); limit: Western.

Justice: fair treatment of all so everybody gets what they deserve.

Fair procedures + fair outcomes; egalitarian (Rawls) vs non-egalitarian.

Case · Eli Lilly

Drug tests on homeless alcoholics for $85/day + room and board (up to $4,500). Big social benefit, but is consent free and informed when people are desperate?

Utilitarian vs rights answer

U Benefits to society + to participants > risks on a few → acceptable, but quantification and distribution problems.

R Desperation undermines free, informed consent → people used as means; violates dignity.

Verdict: problematic as practised; acceptable only with real informed consent and fair risk.

Limits of Western theories

Too…

  • abstract for managers
  • reductionist (one aspect)
  • objective & elitist
  • impersonal
  • rational & codified
  • imperialist

A Virtue ethics

Character & integrity. Right = what a person of virtuous character does; virtue is part of the good life. Learned from culture, codes, role models (Aristotle).

A Feminist ethics

Relationships & responsibility. Empathy, care, harmonious relations, avoiding harm over abstract rules → ethics of care.

A Discourse ethics

Procedures of norm generation. Solve conflicts by rational dialogue on the real experience of all participants. Goal: peaceful settlement (Habermas).

A Postmodern ethics

Empathy & moral impulse. Morality beyond reason; listen to emotions and gut. Practices over principles, "think local, act local", preliminary.

Lens vs prism

Lensone theory → one answer
Prism ✓many theories → many considerations

Exercises: Ethical Dilemma 3 (child labour, Thai partner); Margin Call (Tuld vs Rogers).

Q4 · The trolley problem

Day 220 min + 20 min writingAbsurd Trolley Problems, levels 1–3

Goal: choose for each level and justify with at least 3 theories, then close with pluralism.

The setup

Level 1
Pull: 1 dies instead of 5
Level 2
Pull: 4 die instead of 5
Level 3
Pull: your life savings are destroyed, the 5 live

The core tension

C Consequences: count lives → pull.

D Principles: pulling = actively killing someone not in danger and using them as a means → don't pull.

Killing vs letting die (doing vs allowing) matters to Kant, not to utilitarians.

Bonus depth (beyond slides)

  • Footbridge (J. J. Thomson): push a man off a bridge to stop the trolley. Same 5 vs 1, most refuse → using a person as a means.
  • Greene et al. 2001: personal dilemmas trigger emotional brain areas → ethics is often intuitive (S4: gut + informed brain).
  • Real life: outcomes are uncertain (probability of effect).

Business links

  • Ford Pinto: lives vs costs.
  • Eli Lilly: risk on a few for many.
  • Self-driving car programming.
  • Layoffs to save the company.
  • J&J Tylenol: accept a cost to protect people (level 3 logic).

Answer in 5 moves

  1. Set up: two options, consequences vs principles.
  2. Your choice per level.
  3. Theory that explains it (define it).
  4. Opposite theory + limits.
  5. Pluralist close (prism) + business case.

Model answer (~250 words)

Write yours first, then open

The trolley problem opposes consequentialist theories, which judge an act by its outcomes, and non-consequentialist theories, which judge it by its principles.

Level 1 (5 vs 1): I would pull. Act utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill) explains this: the right act brings the greatest good to the greatest number, and five lives outweigh one. A Kantian would object that I actively kill someone not in danger and use him as a means. Utilitarianism's limit here is the distribution of utility: the minority pays for the majority.

Level 2 (5 vs 4): I would not pull. The gain is one life while I would actively kill four people. The difference between killing and letting die and Kant's human dignity test weigh more when the benefit is so small. It also shows the quantification problem: are lives simply additive?

Level 3 (5 vs my savings): I would pull. Theories converge: utilitarianism (lives outweigh money), Kant (no one is used as a means; "sacrifice property to save lives" can be universalised), rights (property is weaker than life) and virtue ethics (compassion over greed). Only desire-based egoism disagrees.

My answers show ethical pluralism: no single theory is right every time, so theories work best together, like Crane & Matten's "prism". In business, the Ford Pinto case shows the danger of pure cost-benefit reasoning about lives.

S4 · Descriptive ethical theories

Day 340 minCrane & Matten ch.4 · Treviño & Nelson ch.3

Goal: explain why people actually behave (un)ethically: individual factors, the issue, the context, and thinking biases.

Descriptive theory

Describes how ethical decisions are actually made and what influences them.

Normative = should · Descriptive = actually + why.

Is it an ethical decision?

Example: downloading a friend's album.

  1. Significant effects on others
  2. A real choice
  3. Seen as ethical by someone

Rest's 4 stages

Recognise issue→Judge→Intend→Act

Knowing ≠ doing. Managers think mostly in consequences. Influenced by individual + situational factors. Limits: interdependent stages, simplified, US-based.

Individual factors

Indirect effect: age & gender (mixed) · nationality (Hofstede: power distance → less questioning) · education (business students rank lower in moral development) · cognitive moral development, locus of control.

More direct: personal values · integrity · moral imagination.

Locus of control

How far you believe you control events. Internal: "I make things happen". External: "not my fault". Internal → more aware of others, resists peer pressure. Results mixed.

Machiavellianism

Self-interested, deceptive, manipulative, "win at any cost". The Prince: "do good if he can, but commit evil if he must".

Moral disengagement

Switching off guilt through:

  1. Moral justification
  2. Less responsibility: displacement ("boss made me"), diffusion ("team decided"), distortion ("no big deal")
  3. Dehumanisation: "their own fault"

Values · integrity · imagination

Values: stable beliefs about good goals (Rokeach: 70+). Align personal + organisational values (Posner 2010).

Integrity: sticking to consistent principles → whistleblowing (Ellsberg, Watkins/Enron, Markopolos/Madoff, Snowden, Woodford/Olympus).

Moral imagination (Werhane 1998): seeing more options and consequences → win-win solutions.

Moral intensity (Jones 1991)

  1. Magnitude of consequences
  2. Social consensus
  3. Probability of effect
  4. Temporal immediacy
  5. Proximity
  6. Concentration of effect

My Sister Probably Takes Pretty Cats

Moral framing & muteness

Language hides or shows ethics: "cut and paste" → "plagiarised" → "stole ideas".

Moral muteness protects: harmony · efficiency · image of power.

Context factors

Rewards
Bonuses push corner-cutting; unpunished acts repeat.
Authority
People follow bosses' explicit and implicit orders.
Bureau­cracy
Suppresses moral autonomy · instrumental morality · distancing · denial of moral status.
Roles
Functional or hierarchical; Stanford prison (Zimbardo): guards turned abusive, stopped after 6 days.
Norms & culture
Informal standards; Enron's culture of dishonesty.
National
Danish HR manager in Indonesia.

Towards ethical action

People act ethically when they think about harm, use ethical words, see how others would judge it. Ethical people: high moral development, internal locus, idealism; low Machiavellianism and disengagement. Situational factors = managers' best levers.

Films: A Few Good Men ("code red": authority, roles, norms).

S6 · Managing business ethics

Day 430 minCrane & Matten ch.5 · Treviño & Nelson ch.6–7

Goal: know the tools firms use to manage ethics, and why people obey, conform and diffuse responsibility.

US compliance

  • 1987: higher fines.
  • 1991 Sentencing Guidelines: firm convicted for one employee's crime; "carrot and stick": lower fines if good compliance programme.
  • 2000s: ethics officers; FCPA (bribery), Sarbanes-Oxley (accounts).
  • Pfizer $2.3bn (2009), AT&T ~$1bn (2011).

7 requirements

  1. Standards to prevent crime
  2. High-level oversight
  3. No authority to risky people
  4. Communicate & train
  5. Monitor, audit, safe reporting
  6. Consistent discipline
  7. Respond & prevent repeats

Global laws

UK Bribery Act 2010 (bribery anywhere if UK link) · Dodd-Frank 2010 (conflict minerals) · reporting laws: Denmark, France, Japan, Malaysia, UK.

Ethics management

Direct attempt to formally or informally manage ethical issues through policies, practices and programmes.

Components: mission/values · codes · reporting channels (hotlines; limited in France, Germany) · risk analysis · ethics officers · consultants · training · stakeholder consultation · auditing & reporting.

Codes of ethics

Voluntary statement committing an organisation, industry or profession to beliefs, values, actions or employee behaviour.

4 types: organisational · professional · industry · programme.

Research: prevalence (80% EU, 50% Asia large firms) · content (Unilever) · effectiveness (communicated, applied, enforced) · global codes (Japan gifts): core human values + local traditions + context matters.

Communicating ethics

Clear, consistent, credible; align formal + grapevine (70–90% accurate). Know the audience:

Good soldiers
know rules, good compass
Loose cannons
good compass, don't know rules
Grenades
don't care, own agenda

Stakeholder management

Understanding stakeholders' interests and satisfying them in line with the firm's core interests.

Salience (Mitchell et al. 1997): power · legitimacy · urgency.

Antagonistic: Greenpeace vs Lego/Shell 2014. Cooperative: effective but can go wrong (price-fixing).

Collaboration problems: resources · culture clash · "schizophrenia" · coordination · co-optation · accountability · resistance.

Social accounting

Voluntary assessing and communicating of social, ethical and environmental impacts to stakeholders.

vs financial: non-financial, wider audience, voluntary. Materiality matrix.

Why: pressure · risks · stakeholder relations · accountability.

Quality (Zadek 1997): inclusivity · completeness · evolution · management systems · disclosure · external verification · continuous improvement.

Organising ethics

4 orientations: compliance · values · external stakeholders · protection of top management. Evaluate: Otis self-assessment, surveys.

Culture changetop-down, conformity, slow, rarely works
Cultural learningsubcultures, empowerment, moral imagination

Rewards & discipline

  • Reinforcement: people do what's rewarded (risky mortgages).
  • Goal setting can drive unethical acts.
  • Social learning: watching others' rewards.
  • Reward extraordinary ethics.
  • Discipline works if fair and consistent.

Norms · roles · diffusion

"Everyone's doing it": rationalises; pressure to go along ("goody-goody"). Fix: state and enforce rules.

Roles: deindividuation (Zimbardo); conflicting roles (nurse vs doctor).

Diffused responsibility: in groups · by dividing tasks · by psychological distance.

Obedience · Milgram

People obeyed an authority and gave what they thought were dangerous shocks. Learner in the same room → obedience fell to 40%; touching the learner → another ~10% less. Managers must expect people to question wrong orders.

Mock exam

Day 445 min, on paper

Answer each situation on paper with the 5 steps (about 10 min each), then the trolley (15 min). Open the model answers only after.

Situation A

A new analyst is told by her manager to "adjust" the quarterly figures. She hesitates; he says "I take full responsibility." Explain and recommend.

Model answer

Name: obedience to authority (Milgram); displacement of responsibility (moral disengagement); moral framing ("adjust" = falsify).

Explain: most people are at Kohlberg's conventional level and follow superiors (WorldCom). Barrel: hierarchy that doesn't allow questioning.

Judge: Kant: deception fails universality, investors used as means. Utilitarian: short gain, large harm to trust.

Fix: refuse + internal reporting; protected hotline; leaders who expect questions.

Situation B

A multinational shifts profits to a low-tax country. "It's legal," says the CFO. Is it ethical?

Model answer

Law ≠ ethics (grey area). Globalization: deterritorialization, accountability gap. TBL economic dimension includes tax avoidance. Carroll: legal level met, ethical level failed. Friedman: fine. Utilitarian: public services lose. Kant: if all did it, the system collapses. Justice: burden shifts to citizens and SMEs. Fix: transparency, country-by-country reporting, built-in CSR.

Situation C

Two salespeople face the same targets. One lies to customers ("not my fault, targets are crazy"), the other refuses. Why?

Model answer

Apple: locus of control (external vs internal), moral development, Machiavellianism, integrity. Disengagement: displacement onto the system. Barrel: rewards, goal setting, self-interest climate, "the sale is all that counts" norm. Fix: reward how targets are hit (AmEx), fair discipline, training.

Extra case · Jenna's free drinks

Your friend at the student bar undercharges you because she feels mistreated by her boss. Who is wrong? What do you do?

Model answer

Both: she takes the employer's property, you're complicit. Rationalisations: denial of victim, ledger (her), denial of injury (you). Group norm forming (friends). Kant: fails universality, owner used as means. Utilitarian: small gain vs her job and trust. Her grievance explains, doesn't justify. Do: pay full price, talk to her privately.

Extra case · IKEA Saudi catalogue

Model answer

Cultural issue of globalization. Relativism: adapt. Rights: gender equality is a core value. Global code: core human values set an absolute threshold → crossing it is wrong. Commercially right ≠ ethically right; IKEA apologised.

Extra case · Greenpeace vs Lego

Model answer

Greenpeace gained salience: legitimacy, urgency (viral), power (reputation). Lego's refusal → antagonistic relationship; it dropped Shell. Lesson: early dialogue.
